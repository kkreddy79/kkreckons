1 · The runway

161 companies waiting for take-off

Each year Hurun India and ASK Private Wealth rank the private Indian startups most likely to reach a $1 billion valuation. This year they split the list in two.

Gazelles are startups founded since 2000, valued at $500 million to $1 billion, and judged likely to reach unicorn status within three years. India has 51, up from 47. Cheetahs are valued at $200 million to $500 million and are seen as potential unicorns within five years. There are 110, up from 103, and Hurun says that group has nearly doubled since 2021.

Fifteen companies joined the Gazelles this year. Nine climbed up from the Cheetahs, among them rocket maker Agnikul, kids' robotics firm Miko, fan maker Atomberg and farm platform DeHaat. Six arrived directly, including travel fintech Scapia, EV maker Simple Energy, and AI firms Ema and UnifyApps.

The 51 Gazelles have raised about $9 billion between them. The 41 that disclosed results reported FY25 revenue of ₹33,233 crore (about $3.4 billion), growing far faster than India's biggest listed companies (section 4).

How fast are new unicorns really arriving?

India minted 44 unicorns in 2021, when cheap global money flooded into startups. When that money dried up, the number fell to two in 2023 and five in each of 2024 and 2025. Six arrived in the first nine months of 2026.

Now do the sum. If every one of the 161 companies on Hurun's lists made it within five years, that would be about 32 new unicorns a year. That would be a fine result, roughly six times the recent pace, but it would still be less than the 2021 peak. History says many will not make it (section 7).

A fair expectation is somewhere between five and fifteen a year if funding keeps thawing. That is still a lot of billion-dollar companies. It is not two a week.

3 · Who and where

Not just apps any more

Fintech still leads, but the newer names are making rockets, solar modules, chips and electric motorcycles.

Financial services is the biggest group, with 13 of the 51 Gazelles and 17 of the 110 Cheetahs, led by names like lender Finova Capital. The newer names are heavier. Rayzon Solar, which makes solar modules, is among the five most profitable Gazelles. Agnikul builds rockets. The Cheetah list includes eight EV makers and five aerospace and defence firms.

Hurun's Anas Rahman Junaid calls it a shift towards capital-intensive, engineering-led businesses. Matter shows what that looks like. It builds India's first geared electric motorcycle, the Aera, at a plant in Changodar near Ahmedabad that can make 1.2 lakh bikes a year, and holds 111 granted patents.

6 · Patient capital

Who is backing the builders

Deep tech needs money that will wait years for a return. Three new sources are stepping in: the government, India's biggest companies, and founders who have already made their fortunes.

The government · RDI Fund

₹1 lakh crore, lent like patient capital

The Research, Development and Innovation (RDI) scheme was approved by the Cabinet in July 2025 and launched in November 2025. It runs for six years under the Anusandhan National Research Foundation. The fund does not pick companies itself. It passes money to specialist fund managers, such as the Technology Development Board and BIRAC, which choose the projects.

3–4%

Interest on long loans of up to 15 years, with no collateral or personal guarantee. Equity stakes of up to 25% are also allowed.

The first cheques went out on 13 May 2026. Dhruva Space got ₹105 crore for a 500 kg satellite platform, and IISTEM Research got a first ₹50 crore for cell therapies. Drone-helicopter maker Endure Air, battery maker ETRNL Energy and medical-device firm Noccarc Robotics were also in the first group. Of the 22 projects in the first cohort, 14 are startups. Their work includes reusable rockets, quantum-secure communication, chip design and next-generation batteries.

Repayments go back into the pool to be lent again, which makes this a revolving fund rather than a grant. To qualify, the intellectual property and control must stay in India. "India's problem was never ideas. It was patient capital," said Sudhir Mehta, who sits on the investment committee of the Technology Development Board. India spends only about 0.64% of GDP on R&D, and private industry pays for just over a third of that.

India's big companies

IT giants buy into Indian AI

In June, HCLTech put $150 million into Sarvam AI. That led the $234 million first close of a $300 million round, which made Sarvam, the Bengaluru maker of Indian-language AI models, a unicorn at $1.5 billion. The two are building what they call a sovereign AI platform for businesses.

Cognizant is reported (Economic Times, 28 October) to be in talks to invest about $300 million at a $3.5–4 billion valuation, more than double. Neither company has confirmed this.

Others are writing cheques too. Zoho invested in a ₹107 crore round for chip startup Netrasemi. Wipro added $200 million to its venture arm in 2025. The India Deep Tech Investment Alliance, with Nvidia, Qualcomm Ventures, Info Edge Ventures, Premji Invest and more than ten VC firms, now has over $1.8 billion pledged to Indian chips, space, quantum and robotics.

Founders who invest

Founders who reinvest their winnings

Kunal Shah (CRED) is India's most prolific founder-investor. By June 2026, Tracxn data showed 299 startups backed, typically around $100,000 each, with 14 now unicorns. On a 2024 count, the next most active were Anupam Mittal, Kunal Bahl and Rohit Bansal.

Nithin Kamath's Zerodha sets aside 10% of earnings for Rainmatter, which has put over ₹1,500 crore into 160+ startups, 44 of them in climate. It takes no board seats.

Deepinder Goyal (Zomato) co-founded LAT Aerospace with about $20 million of his own money to build a short-runway hybrid aircraft and Indian gas-turbine engines. In February it bought an anti-drone startup.

The Groww co-founders sold about ₹270 crore of shares in May and plan a ₹400–500 crore fund of their own money for consumer and deep-tech startups.

The fine print. RDI money is a loan or an equity stake, not a grant, so the earliest lab-stage research is excluded. Most of the ₹1 lakh crore has not yet been committed. Corporate investors want strategic returns, and Reliance wrote off all ₹1,645 crore it put into Dunzo. Even the best angel portfolios have misses: several of Kunal Shah's startups have shut down.

The startups that listed between 2021 and 2024 are mostly below their IPO price. FirstCry, Swiggy and Ola Electric are down by more than half. Ola hit ₹22.25 in March and is now raising money in a rights issue priced at ₹27.

The 2025 class has done far better. Groww, Lenskart and Meesho are trading well above their IPO prices, and Shadowfax has more than doubled despite listing below its issue price in January.

Shutdowns are rising. Inc42 counted about 25 notable startup closures in 2025, double the 12 of 2024. By January 2026, 6,789 government-recognised startups had shut. Startups cut more than 9,500 jobs across 44 companies in 2025, and about a third of those companies closed entirely.

What founders are up against

Eight reasons not every Gazelle will make it

  • 01Regulatory whiplash. One rule can erase a business model. The RBI's 2024 action on Paytm Payments Bank and the 2025 real-money gaming ban both did.
  • 02Governance. Byju's, GoMechanic, Builder.ai and Gensol all turned on what founders told investors and lenders.
  • 03Profit is still rare. Only 15 of the 51 Gazelles are profitable. Their combined value is about ten times their disclosed revenue.
  • 04Foreign money left. Tiger Global made 55 Indian investments in 2021 and one in 2026. SoftBank went from 17 to two.
  • 05Deep tech takes years. Skyroot took eight years from founding to orbit. Matter, founded in 2019, began deliveries only after repeated delays.
  • 06Lists are not destinies. In 2023 almost a fifth of Hurun's previous Gazelles and Cheetahs dropped off or were downgraded. In five years, only 15 Cheetahs have become unicorns.
  • 07Who counts as a job. Hurun's headcounts appear to be payroll staff. Delivery riders at firms like Shadowfax are usually not counted, and no source splits out gig work.
  • 08Self-reported totals. The 25 lakh startup jobs figure is what startups declared when registering. It does not subtract jobs lost when they shut.
