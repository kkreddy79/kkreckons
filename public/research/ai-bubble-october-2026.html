KKReckons research brief · 28 October 2026

China popped. America is leaking.

The AI boom is being tested in two very different ways. China's AI shares have crashed. In the US, the strain is showing up in the debt of the most heavily borrowed builders, while the biggest spenders keep spending. This brief checks the numbers behind this week's headlines and sets out what to watch.

1 · China

The bubble that already burst

Reuters reports that China's AI rally ran higher than any in the West and has now unwound fast. AI stocks make up 42% of China's market value and 57% of turnover, Goldman Sachs estimates, so few small investors escaped.

Traders named no single trigger. They pointed to stretched prices, tax bills and the threat of US and other bans on Chinese equipment in data centres. Beijing's 9 October guidelines on high-tech growth told officials to avoid bubbles and herd-like investment. The securities regulator has announced no rescue.

2 · US credit

Where the air is getting out

Neoclouds rent out GPU capacity they bought with borrowed money. When rates rise, their sums get worse even if customers keep coming. That is why the stress shows first in their bonds and credit default swaps (CDS), the price of insuring their debt.

3 · The maths

A $4.9tn hole, on Gartner's model

Gartner estimates data-centre owners need $8.5tn of cumulative revenue by 2030 to cover announced investment, financing costs and a 12% return on capital. It sees revenue attributable to AI data centres reaching no more than $3.6tn.

4 · Leak or burst?

Ed Yardeni's slow-puncture thesis

The veteran strategist told CNBC that air is coming out of the bubble without it bursting. His case: the bond market is rationing money to weak borrowers faster than rating agencies, so weak players fail or sell while stronger ones carry on.

Why it might stay a slow leak

  • The biggest spenders fund most capex from cash flow.
  • Cloud backlogs keep growing: nearly $1.7tn at the big three.
  • Stronger firms can buy weak ones' assets cheaply.

Why it could still burst

  • Credit is where past bubbles broke.
  • A December Fed hike would push the trigger dial up.
  • If Big Tech trims spending, neocloud debts get harder to pay, and chipmakers feel it next.
5 · History

Investors lose; the rails stay

Past infrastructure manias destroyed capital but left assets that were used later. GPUs may be the exception.

UK · 1840s

Railway mania

~1/3

of about £200m invested (roughly 40% of GDP) was lost to investors, Andrew Odlyzko estimates. The network was built and used.

US · 1996–2002

Telecom fibre

~95%

of laid fibre was reported dark in 2001. Global Crossing went bankrupt with $12.4bn of debt. Much of the fibre was lit later.

Now

AI data centres

3–5 yrs

is roughly how long a GPU stays competitive. Unlike fibre, stranded chips may age out before a second life. This is our inference.

6 · The wider economy

Is AI crowding out other investment?

Paul Krugman says it is. Columbia's Neeraj Kaushal points out that total US investment has barely moved, so AI may be reshuffling capital rather than starving the rest.

Scale 0–25%. The NY Fed finds investment was reallocated rather than expanded: factories, other buildings and housing weakened as AI rose. Kaushal's column dates the later period from 2022; the NY Fed post uses 2023 to Q1 2026.

Signs of crowding out

  • Non-AI business investment fell as AI rose.
  • Data centres compete for power, land and builders; the BIS flags higher electricity costs.
  • Heavy borrowing adds to pressure on rates.

Signs it isn't

  • The overall investment rate is flat.
  • Business saving has held up because the spenders are cash-rich.
  • Foreign capital can flow in, as Kaushal notes.

8 · The India angle

For India, AI has been a bystander trade

Foreign investors have pulled money from India to chase AI and chip stocks in the US, Taiwan and Korea. When AI sold off, Indian IT briefly became the place to hide.

IT was the only large sector with foreign inflows (₹2,599 crore) while foreign investors sold ₹47,955 crore overall. Year-to-date foreign outflows reached a record ₹3.08 lakh crore by 12 Oct. The IT rally faded on 26 Oct.

The real risk for Indian IT

  • AI is pushing prices down: HCLTech's CEO says the industry is in the early phases of deflation.
  • Infosys cut its growth guidance to 1.5–3%, even as AI reached 10.5% of revenue.

The lesson from China

  • The danger is retail money piling into a theme officials seem to bless.
  • India's closest parallels are small and mid-caps and AI data-centre listings, not the Nifty.
  • Goldman's basket of 42 Indian AI enablers is up about 60%.

The other side

The bull case is not foolish

The spenders are among the most cash-generative companies ever, and unlike in 2000, business saving has not collapsed. Cloud backlogs keep growing, and Anthropic's revenue rose roughly 14-fold in the first half. Gartner's ceiling is a model that bulls dispute. Even in the bear case, history says the infrastructure gets used and the losses fall on leveraged early financiers rather than the whole economy.

KKReckons' read: a credit event at the edges, not yet a systemic bust. But credit is exactly where past manias turned, which is why the run from 28 October to 9 December matters.
